01

Twenty-seven turbines and one windy day

This is a wind farm: 27 turbines spread over rolling fields. Each one is a Vestas V150, and at full power it makes 4.2 megawatts (MW). A megawatt is a million watts, so together the farm can make about 113 MW. It is sized like Målarberget in Sweden, a real farm of 27 of these turbines that has run since 2021.

The turbines stand in rows across the prevailing wind, the direction the wind most often blows from. Here that is the west. Engineers measure the gaps in rotor diameters, written D, and one D here is 150 m. Rows sit 7 D apart, or 1,050 m, and turbines in a row sit 4 D apart, or 600 m. Each row is shifted sideways, so no turbine stands right behind one in the row in front.

From above it looks crowded, but most of the land is still fields. Only about 0.3 hectares per megawatt is actually built on, and most of that is access roads, not turbine bases. A hectare is a square 100 m on each side. The power runs through collector cables to a substation, where it heads for the grid.

Målarberget's real layout is not published, so the one you see is this model's own. The model also plays a made-up day of wind, on a loop. It is not an average day: it fits a calm night, a breezy morning, a full-power afternoon and an evening storm into 24 hours. The clock runs hundreds of times faster than real time, but every rotor turns at its real speed. Roads, cables and wind arrows are drawn wider than real so you can see them from kilometres away, and the light does not follow the clock.

02

Blades that fly in circles

Up close, one turbine is huge. The steel tower lifts the hub 105 m above the ground, and the rotor, the hub with its three blades, is 150 m across. At the top of its turn, a blade tip reaches about 180 m, almost twice as high as Big Ben. Up there the wind blows faster than near the ground, as chapter 5 shows.

Each blade is about 74 m long, almost the wingspan of an Airbus A380, the biggest airliner. It is made of carbon fibre and glass fibre set in epoxy, a hard plastic glue. A blade is not a sail that the wind pushes. The wind flows over it as over a plane's wing, and the curved shape creates lift, a force across the wind. Lift pulls each blade round, and the rotor turns.

At full speed the rotor makes only about 10 turns a minute, or 10 rpm (revolutions per minute). That is one turn every 6 seconds, which looks lazy. But the tips travel a huge circle, so they move at about 80 m/s, close to 300 km/h. Drag the wind slider to see the rotor speed up with the wind, or pick Follow the day to give the wind back to the clock. In this model the tips move 8 times faster than the wind until the rotor reaches its top speed.

The circle the blades sweep is the swept area: about 17,700 m², the size of about 2.5 football pitches. Only the wind that passes through this circle can turn the rotor. The lines of air round the rotor are a drawing, since real air is invisible. The transformer stands at the foot of the tower in this model, because where the V150 keeps it is not published. For the same reason, the slight tilt of the rotor is not drawn.

03

Inside the nacelle

The long box behind the hub is the nacelle, 12.8 m long. Pick Cover off to look inside. It holds the machines that turn the rotor's slow, strong spin into electricity. They all stand on the bedplate, a heavy cast iron frame, and a cooler on the roof carries their heat away.

In the hub, each blade sits on a bearing so it can twist. Three pitch cylinders, one per blade, twist them with oil pressure. The blade's angle to the wind is its pitch, and it sets how much the wind can grab. Behind the hub, the main shaft turns in its main bearing at the rotor's own slow speed, about 10 rpm.

A generator needs to spin fast, so a gearbox sits in between. It has two planetary stages, where small gears circle round a central one, and a helical stage with slanted teeth. Together they make the shaft about 140 times faster, up to about 1,485 rpm. The generator is an induction generator, with copper windings and no magnets. All its power, at 800 volts, passes through the converter, which reshapes it to match the grid.

On the shaft sits a disc brake. It is not what stops the turbine in a storm: the blades do that by turning edge-on to the wind. The brake only holds the rotor once it is slow, to park it. On top of the tower, yaw drives turn the whole nacelle to face the wind. In this model it follows the wind exactly, because the V150's real turning speed is not published.

04

How much wind a turbine can catch

Below 3 m/s, the cut-in speed, the rotor waits. Above it, power climbs fast, because the power in the wind grows with the speed cubed: speed times speed times speed. Double the wind, and it carries eight times the power. So the turbine makes about 0.3 MW at 4 m/s, about 2.5 MW at 8 m/s and its full 4.2 MW from about 12 m/s. Drag the wind slider and watch the dot move along the curve.

No turbine can catch all of it. To take energy, the rotor has to slow the air down, yet the air must still flow out behind it. The physicist Albert Betz showed that even a perfect rotor can take at most 59.3 % of the power. That is the Betz limit. This turbine's best is just under half, at about 7 to 8 m/s, and the chart shows how close it comes.

Above full power the wind brings more than the generator can take. So the pitch cylinders turn the blades a little out of the wind, and most of it slips past. At 15 m/s the turbine keeps only about 12 % of the power in the wind. The pitch angles shown above full power are this model's own, since the V150's are not published. Above 20 m/s, power starts to ramp down.

At the cut-out speed, about 22 to 25 m/s, the blades turn edge-on to the wind. This is feathering: the rotor slows on its own, and then the disc brake parks it. The turbine starts again only once the wind has dropped by about 2 m/s. In this model all 27 turbines stop together at 24.5 m/s and restart at 22.5 m/s. The stop and the restart each take 20 minutes of the clock, longer than real, so you can watch them.

05

Why turbines stand so far apart

A turbine takes energy out of the wind, so the air behind it moves slower. This trail of slow, stirred air is called a wake. Switch on Wakes (K) to see a plume behind every turning rotor. Wakes are strongest in moderate wind, when the rotor holds back the air the hardest. Above full power the blades let more wind slip past, and the wakes fade.

This model draws wakes with a simple, widely used recipe, the Jensen model. It treats each wake as a cone that widens and slowly fills back up with faster air. How fast it widens is set by one number, 0.075 here, the usual value for land. At 8 m/s, a turbine 7 D downwind still meets air about 13 % slower. Because power follows the wind cubed, that costs it about a third of its power.

Over a year, wakes cost a farm about 5 to 15 % of its energy. A Vestas example farm of 24 of these turbines loses about 6 %. For a single wind direction, the loss can pass 30 %. That is why rows usually stand 5 to 9 D apart along the wind and 3 to 5 D apart across it. Try 5, 7 and 9 D: wider gaps lose less wind but need more land.

Wind also grows with height, because the ground drags on the air near it. Switch on Airflow (F) to see the profile: the top of the rotor meets faster wind than the bottom. The plumes and arrows are drawings of invisible air. In this model the ground is flat, a stopped rotor leaves no wake, and wakes that overlap add up in a simple way.

06

The road to the grid

Each generator makes power at 800 volts (V). After the converter, the turbine's own transformer raises the voltage. Higher voltage carries the same power with less current, so less of it is lost as heat in the cables. Switch on Cables (C) to follow the power from the turbines to the grid.

Collector cables at 36,000 volts, or 36 kilovolts (kV), link the turbines to the substation. There another transformer raises the voltage to 110 kV for the grid line. These voltages come from a Vestas example farm in Germany.

At full power, one turbine makes 4.2 megawatt hours (MWh) every hour. But the wind is not always strong, so over a year a turbine averages about 40 % of its full power. That share is the capacity factor. In a year, one turbine makes about 14,700 MWh, enough for about 1,400 US homes. The model's made-up day is windier than a year's average, so its capacity factor comes out higher.

Making a turbine costs energy and carbon too. Over its life, it adds about 7 g of CO2e for each kilowatt hour (kWh) it makes, where CO2e counts every greenhouse gas as if it were carbon dioxide. It pays back the energy used to build it in about 8 months. It is designed for 20 years and often runs longer, though its blades are still hard to recycle. Pick a calm, typical or windy site to see how much the everyday wind matters.
