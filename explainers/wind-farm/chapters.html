01

Twenty-seven turbines and one windy day

This is a wind farm: 27 turbines spread over rolling fields. Each one is a Vestas V150, and at full power it makes 4.2 megawatts (MW). A megawatt is a million watts, so the whole farm can make about 113 MW. That matches Målarberget in Sweden, a real farm of 27 of these turbines that has run since 2021.

The turbines stand in rows across the prevailing wind, the direction the wind blows from most often. Here that is the west. Engineers measure the gaps in rotor widths, written D. One D is the width of a rotor from tip to tip, 150 m here. The rows are 7 D apart, or 1,050 m. Within a row, turbines are 4 D apart, or 600 m. Each row is shifted a little sideways, so no turbine hides right behind another.

From above it looks crowded, but most of the land is still fields. Only about 0.3 hectares per megawatt is actually built on. A hectare is a square 100 m on each side. Most of that built land is access roads, not turbine bases. Collector cables gather the power from every turbine and carry it to a substation, where it joins the grid.

The real layout of Målarberget is not published, so this one is the model's own. The day of wind is made up too, and it plays on a loop. It is not an average day: it packs a calm night, a breezy morning, a full-power afternoon and an evening storm into 24 hours. The clock runs hundreds of times faster than real time, yet every rotor turns at its real speed. Roads, cables and wind arrows are drawn wider than they are, so you can see them from kilometres away. The light stays the same at every hour.

02

Blades that fly in circles

Up close, one turbine is huge. The rotor is three blades fixed to a round hub, and it is 150 m across. The steel tower lifts the hub 105 m above the ground. At the top of its turn, a blade tip reaches about 180 m, almost twice the height of Big Ben. Up there the wind is faster than near the ground, as chapter 5 shows.

Each blade is about 74 m long, almost the wingspan of an Airbus A380, the biggest airliner. It is made of carbon and glass fibre set in epoxy, a hard plastic glue. The wind does not push a blade like a sail. It flows over the blade the way it flows over a plane's wing. The curved shape creates lift, a force at right angles to the wind. Lift pulls each blade round, and the rotor turns.

At full speed the rotor turns only about 10 times a minute, or 10 rpm (revolutions per minute). That is one turn every 6 seconds, which looks lazy. But the tips travel round a huge circle, so they move at about 80 m/s, close to 300 km/h. Drag the wind slider and watch the rotor speed up with the wind. Pick Follow the day to hand the wind back to the clock. In this model the tips move 8 times as fast as the wind, until the rotor reaches its top speed.

The circle the blades sweep is the swept area: about 17,700 m², or about 2.5 football pitches. Only wind that passes through this circle can turn the rotor. The lines of air round the rotor are a drawing, since real air is invisible. The turbine's transformer raises the voltage of its power for the cables. Where the V150 keeps it is not published, so this model puts it at the foot of the tower. For the same reason, the rotor's slight tilt is not drawn.

03

Inside the nacelle

The box behind the hub is the nacelle, 12.8 m long. Pick Cover off to look inside. It holds the machines that turn the rotor's slow, strong spin into electricity. They all stand on the bedplate, a heavy cast iron frame. A cooler on the roof carries their heat away.

The angle of a blade to the wind is its pitch. It sets how much of the wind the blade can grab. Each blade sits in the hub on a bearing, so it can twist. Three pitch cylinders, one per blade, twist the blades with oil pressure. Behind the hub, the main shaft turns in its main bearing at the rotor's slow speed, about 10 rpm.

The generator needs a fast spin, so a gearbox sits in between. It has two planetary stages, where small gears circle round a central one, and one helical stage with slanted teeth. Together they spin the output about 140 times faster, up to about 1,485 rpm. The generator is an induction generator: copper windings, no magnets. Its power comes out at 800 volts and all of it passes through the converter. The converter reshapes the power so it keeps in step with the grid.

On the shaft sits a disc brake. It is not what stops the turbine in a storm: the blades do that by turning edge-on to the wind. The brake only holds the rotor still once it has slowed down, to park it. On top of the tower, yaw drives turn the whole nacelle to face the wind. This turning is called yaw. In this model the nacelle follows the wind exactly, because the V150's real turning speed is not published.

04

How much wind a turbine can catch

The rotor waits until the wind reaches 3 m/s, the cut-in speed. Then power climbs fast. The power in the wind grows with the speed cubed: speed times speed times speed. Double the wind, and it carries eight times the power. So the turbine makes about 0.3 MW at 4 m/s, about 2.5 MW at 8 m/s and its full 4.2 MW from about 12 m/s. Drag the wind slider and watch the dot move along the curve.

No turbine can catch all of that power. To take energy, the rotor must slow the air down, but the air still has to flow away behind it. If the rotor stopped the air completely, no new air could get through. The physicist Albert Betz showed that even a perfect rotor can take at most 59.3 % of the wind's power. That is the Betz limit. This turbine's best is just under half, at about 7 to 8 m/s. The chart shows how close it comes.

Above full power the wind brings more than the generator can take. So the pitch cylinders turn the blades a little out of the wind, and most of the wind slips past. At 15 m/s the turbine keeps only about 12 % of the power in the wind. The pitch angles this model shows above full power are its own, since the V150's are not published. Above 20 m/s, the turbine starts to ramp its power down.

At the cut-out speed, about 22 to 25 m/s, the wind is too strong to work in. The blades turn edge-on to the wind. This is feathering: the rotor slows down on its own, and then the disc brake parks it. The turbine starts again only once the wind has dropped by about 2 m/s. In this model all 27 turbines stop together at 24.5 m/s and restart at 22.5 m/s. Stopping and restarting each take 20 minutes on the clock, longer than in real life, so you can watch them.

05

Why turbines stand so far apart

A turbine takes energy out of the wind, so the air behind it moves slower. This trail of slow, stirred-up air is called a wake, like the trail behind a boat. Switch on Wakes (K) to see a plume behind every turning rotor. Wakes are strongest in moderate wind, when the rotor holds back the air the most. Above full power the blades let more wind slip past, and the wakes fade.

This model draws wakes with a simple, widely used recipe, the Jensen model. It treats each wake as a cone that widens and slowly fills back up with faster air. One number sets how fast it widens: 0.075 here, the usual value on land. At 8 m/s, a turbine 7 D downwind still meets air about 13 % slower. Power follows the speed cubed, so that costs it about a third of its power.

Over a year, wakes cost a farm about 5 to 15 % of its energy. In a Vestas example farm of 24 of these turbines, the loss is about 6 %. When the wind comes from one unlucky direction, the loss can pass 30 %. That is why rows usually stand 5 to 9 D apart along the wind, and turbines 3 to 5 D apart across it. Try 5, 7 and 9 D: wider gaps lose less to wakes but need more land.

Wind also gets stronger with height, because the ground drags on the air near it. Switch on Airflow (F) to see this: the top of the rotor meets faster wind than the bottom. The plumes and arrows are only drawings. In this model the ground is flat, a stopped rotor leaves no wake, and overlapping wakes add up in a simple way.

06

The road to the grid

Each generator makes power at 800 volts (V). After the converter, the turbine's own transformer raises the voltage. At a higher voltage the same power needs less current, so the cables waste less of it as heat. Switch on Cables (C) to follow the power from the turbines to the grid.

Collector cables at 36,000 volts, or 36 kilovolts (kV), link the turbines to the substation. There another transformer raises the voltage again, to 110 kV, for the grid line. These voltages come from a Vestas example farm in Germany.

At full power, one turbine makes 4.2 megawatt hours (MWh) every hour. But the wind is not always strong. Over a year, a turbine averages about 40 % of its full power. That share is its capacity factor. In a year, one turbine makes about 14,700 MWh, enough for about 1,400 US homes. The model's made-up day is windier than an average day, so its capacity factor comes out higher.

Building a turbine costs energy and carbon too. Over its whole life, it causes about 7 g of CO2e for each kilowatt hour (kWh) it makes. CO2e counts every greenhouse gas as if it were carbon dioxide. A turbine pays back the energy used to build it in about 8 months. It is designed for 20 years and often runs longer, though its blades are still hard to recycle. Pick a calm, typical or windy site to see how much the everyday wind matters.
