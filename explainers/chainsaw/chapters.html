01

One engine, one loop of teeth

This is a chainsaw, a professional saw like the Husqvarna 572 XP. Its engine sits inside the orange engine housing. A flat steel guide bar sticks out 40 cm in front, and a loop of sharp teeth, the saw chain, runs round its edge. Your right hand holds the rear handle and squeezes the throttle trigger. Your left hand holds the front handle, just behind the front hand guard.

An engine's size is the space its piston sweeps, measured in cubic centimetres (cm³). A homeowner's saw has about 30 to 50 cm³ and makes 1.4 to 2.4 kilowatts (kW) of power. A professional saw like this one has about 70 to 80 cm³ and makes 4.3 to 5 kW. Yet it weighs only a little more: this one is 6.6 kg without its bar and chain. Saws were not always this light. Stihl's gasoline saw of 1929 weighed 46 kg and needed two people. Only around 1950 did saws become light enough for one.

This page follows one turn of the crankshaft, the shaft the engine spins. At full power it turns about 9,900 times a minute, or 9,900 rpm (revolutions per minute). So one turn takes about 6 ms, six thousandths of a second. On a screen that is just a blur, so the model plays the turn 64 to 2,048 times slower than life. You can change how slow under Playback.

Here is the story of one turn. A spark fires, and burning gas pushes the piston down. The piston turns the crankshaft, the crankshaft turns the clutch, and the clutch turns a toothed wheel that pulls the chain. Each tooth on the chain takes a chip of wood. The model keeps some things simple: the throttle sets the engine speed in a straight line from idle to full power, while a real saw speeds up and slows down with the cut too. Drag the throttle slider and watch the chain speed follow.

02

One spark every turn

The engine is a two-stroke. A stroke is one trip of the piston, up or down. A two-stroke does all its work in just two strokes, which is one turn of the crankshaft. So the spark plug fires once every turn.

The piston does two jobs at once. Its top works in the cylinder, where the fuel burns. Its underside pumps the crankcase, the closed box round the crankshaft below. As the piston rises, the crankcase gets bigger and draws fresh mixture in. As it falls, the crankcase gets smaller and squeezes that mixture.

Follow the turn on the scrubber. Near the top, the spark lights the squeezed mixture of fuel and air. The burning gas pushes the piston down, and the connecting rod turns that push into a spin. This is the power stroke. On the way down, the piston uncovers openings in the cylinder wall, called ports. The exhaust port opens first, and the burnt gas rushes out to the muffler.

A moment later, the transfer ports open too. They are channels that lead up from the crankcase. The squeezed fresh mixture flows up through them and sweeps the last burnt gas out of the exhaust port. This is called scavenging. On the way back up, the ports close and the piston squeezes the new mixture. At the same time, underneath, the crankcase draws the next mixture in through the intake.

At 9,900 rpm, the spark fires 165 times a second. It jumps a gap of 0.5 mm between the plug's two tips. Makers do not publish exactly when the ports open or when the spark fires, so the model's timing is its own. That is why the scrubber shows how far into the turn you are, not an exact angle. Cutaway (C) opens the engine, and Gas and oil flow (F) shows where the gases go.

03

Fuel that runs at any angle

Most saws mix their fuel and air in a carburettor. Air comes in through the air filter, and the carburettor adds a fine spray of fuel to it. The throttle valve is a small flap that the trigger turns. It sets how much mixture can flow into the engine. Pick Idle, Half or Full and watch the flap open.

On a cold morning, the choke closes off some of the air. That makes the mixture richer in fuel, so the cold engine starts more easily. Once it runs, you open the choke again. The fuel itself waits in the fuel tank under the rear handle.

You hold a saw at every angle, even on its side. Many engines measure their fuel with a float in a small bowl, which only works upright. So a saw's carburettor measures fuel with a diaphragm, a thin flexible sheet, and a small pump feeds it. The pump is driven by the crankcase itself. Every turn, the pressure in the crankcase rises and falls, and the pulse line carries that beat to the pump. That is why the engine keeps running at any angle.

A two-stroke has no pool of oil under its crankshaft. Its oil travels with the fuel instead. You mix two-stroke oil into the gasoline at 50 to 1: 100 ml of oil in 5 litres of gasoline. The oil keeps the moving parts slippery on its way through the engine. Not every saw has a carburettor, though: some professional saws use electronic fuel injection instead.

04

Why the chain stops at idle

Between the crankshaft and the chain sits a centrifugal clutch. Its weights, the clutch shoes, spin with the crankshaft, and springs hold them in. Around them is a steel cup, the clutch drum. The drum carries the sprocket, the toothed wheel that drives the chain. As the engine speeds up, the spin flings the shoes outward against the drum, and friction makes the drum turn with them.

At idle, about 2,700 rpm, the springs win and the shoes stay in. The drum stands still, so the chain does not move. That is how it should be: at idle, the chain must not run. On this saw, the shoes grip at 3,700 rpm. Drag the throttle slowly past that point and watch the chain start. In the model, the clutch grips fully the instant the engine passes 3,700 rpm, so the chain jumps straight to about 8 m/s; a real clutch slips for a moment as the shoes bite.

The sprocket has 7 teeth, and each one moves the chain by two pitches, two link spacings. So one turn moves the chain about 133 mm. At 9,900 rpm, that is 22 metres per second (m/s), about 79 km/h. This is the speed where the engine makes its full power, and it works near it while cutting. With nothing to cut, saws can rev higher, to a built-in limit of 12,500 to 14,000 rpm.

A saw starts with the throttle held a little open. That is fast enough for the clutch to grip, so you put the chain brake on before you start. If the chain is held still while the engine races, the clutch slips and gets very hot. The oil pump sends oil to the bar only while the chain runs: at idle, no oil flows.

05

Teeth that take chips

A saw chain is built from three kinds of parts. Drive links hang down into a groove in the bar, and the sprocket's teeth push them along. Cutters are the teeth, one leaning left, the next leaning right. Small plates called tie straps join them, held by rivets.

Each cutter has a flat top plate and a side plate. Where they meet is a sharp corner that slices the wood. Just in front of each cutter stands a small hump, the depth gauge. It sits 0.65 mm lower than the cutter's top, and that gap sets how thick a chip the cutter can take. File the depth gauges too low and the cutters bite too deep, which makes kickback more likely.

A chain's size is its pitch: take the distance across three rivets in a row and halve it. This chain has a 3/8 inch pitch, 9.525 mm. A chain like this one on a 40 cm bar has 55 drive links and is about 1.05 m long. It carries about 27 cutters, one for every two drive links.

At 22 m/s, the chain runs round the bar about 21 times a second. That sends about 570 cutters past any one point every second, and each takes a chip. Pick Idle, Half or Full to compare. In the cut, the chain under the bar runs back from the bar nose toward the saw, so the chips fly out behind the cut.

All this time, the chain slides along the steel bar at full speed. Without oil, the rubbing would soon make the bar burning hot. So the oil pump drips bar oil onto the chain, and the chain carries it round the bar. On a small Stihl saw, the bar oil runs out at about the same time as the fuel, so you can fill both tanks together.

06

The upper quarter of the nose

The most dangerous part of a saw is the top quarter of the bar nose, the kickback zone. Kickback zone (K) paints it red. If the moving chain touches wood there, the cutters can catch. The bar is then flung up and back toward you in a flash. This is kickback, and measurements show a gasoline saw can spin at over 1,000° a second.

A person needs about 0.1 to 0.2 s to react. By then, the bar can already have swung far enough to reach you. So reflexes alone cannot save you. Kickback has two cousins too. If the chain on the bottom of the bar snags, pull-in drags the saw forward. If the chain on top snags, pushback drives the saw straight back.

The chain brake is the answer. A steel brake band wraps round the clutch drum. When your left hand pushes the front hand guard forward, a spring snaps the band tight. The band grips the drum, and the chain stops within a fraction of a second. In a strong kickback, the guard can trip by itself. The saw flips up, but the guard keeps moving by inertia, the way a moving thing tends to keep going, so it swings forward. This is the inertia brake.

Pick Show a kickback to see a strong one, slowed down and smaller than life. Watch the guard snap forward and the band clamp the drum. In the model, the chain stops at once. Not every kickback trips the brake on its own, though: a milder one may need your hand. The brake lowers the risk, but it cannot stop the kickback itself. Two more helpers: the chain catcher, a hook under the bar, catches a chain that breaks or jumps off, and low-kickback chain, sold with a green label in North America, lowers the risk too.

In the United States in 1979, kickback caused nearly one in four chainsaw injuries. Today, about 70 people a day still go to emergency rooms there with chainsaw injuries. A saw is also loud: about 104 to 107 decibels (dB(A)) at your ear. Without hearing protection, 107 dB(A) is safe for only about 3 minutes a day. So wear ear protection, keep both hands on the saw and keep the nose clear.
