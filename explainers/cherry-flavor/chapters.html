01

What the tongue can tell

Take a spoon of sour-cherry ice cream and let it rest on your tongue. Your tongue can tell you five things, the five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Umami is the savory taste of broth and cheese. Each taste has its own kind of taste cell. You may have seen a map of the tongue with one zone per taste. That map is a myth: every part of the tongue with taste buds senses all five.

The tiny bumps on your tongue are called papillae. Many of them hold taste buds, and you have a few thousand. Each bud is a small cluster of about 50 to 100 cells. Sweet is caught by a single receptor. It is built from two parts that work as a pair. So from this ice cream, your tongue reports sweet, and a little sour from the tart cherry.

What about the cold, and the soft, creamy feel? Those are not tastes at all. Cold is felt by temperature nerves in your mouth. Creaminess is felt by touch. Both tell you a lot about the ice cream, but nothing about cherry.

Cold does change taste, though. Ice cream is usually served at about −12 °C, and your mouth is about 37 °C. Below about 12 °C, sweet tastes weaker. Cold also makes sweetness fade faster. Watch the readouts. Once the bite has melted, sweet grows and cold fades. Still, nothing on the tongue says cherry.

The seconds on the timeline are only a guide. Nobody has measured how long a spoonful takes to melt in the mouth. The model splits one bite into six steps so you can follow it. In a real mouth, the steps overlap. The way the bite warms up is the model's own sketch too. Pick Head or Molecules to look at the same bite another way.

02

Two ways into the nose

So where does the cherry come from? From your nose. Smells can reach it in two ways. When you sniff, air carries them in through your nostrils. This first way is called orthonasal smell, which means smelling from the front.

The second way starts inside your mouth. As the bite melts, aroma rises from it. Each time you breathe out, the air carries that aroma up behind the palate, the roof of your mouth, and into your nose from the back. This is retronasal smell, smelling from behind. It happens most while you chew and swallow.

Both ways end at the same smell patch, high up in the nasal cavity, the space inside your nose. Yet smell from inside the mouth feels as if it came from the tongue. So we call it taste, even though it is smell. The psychologist Paul Rozin wrote about these two sides of smell in 1982.

You can test this with a fruity sweet. Pinch your nose shut and chew. You notice only sweet, and maybe a little sour. Now let go, and the fruit appears within a moment. Pick Nose pinched to try it on the model. Most of what we call cherry flavor comes from smell.

03

A smell is a pattern

The smell patch is packed with millions of smell neurons, nerve cells that sense smell. Their tips reach into a thin layer of mucus. Each neuron carries receptors of one kind. A receptor works like a tiny catcher. When a molecule it fits lands on it, the neuron fires and sends a signal up to the smell bulb.

Your nose has about 400 types of smell receptor. Compare that with sweet, where one receptor does the whole job. Richard Axel and Linda Buck found the smell receptors, and in 2004 they won the Nobel Prize for it.

There is no single receptor for each smell. One receptor type picks up several different molecules. And one molecule sets off several receptor types. So every smell is a pattern of receptors firing together, a bit like a chord on a piano. Linda Buck's team showed this in 1999.

04

Cherry taken apart

How do scientists find out what makes cherry smell of cherry? A machine called a gas chromatograph pulls the aroma apart, one molecule after another. A mass spectrometer then names each one. At a sniffing port, a person smells each molecule as it comes out and says which ones really smell. Diluting the sample again and again shows which smells are the strongest.

In sour cherry, this found seven key odorants, the molecules that carry the smell. The main one is benzaldehyde. With it come green, leafy notes such as hexenal, flowery linalool and clove-like eugenol. Sweet cherry is different. One study found 97 aroma molecules in it, and only 18 were strong enough to smell. In sweet cherry, green notes lead, together with beta-ionone, which smells of violets.

Benzaldehyde, C7H6O, is a ring of six carbon atoms with a small group of atoms on the side. It smells of bitter almond and cherry. It is strong, too: one drop could be smelled in about a bathtub of water. It is also the smell of marzipan, which is often flavored with bitter almond oil. That oil is almost pure benzaldehyde.

Cherry sweets lean on benzaldehyde and leave out the green and flowery notes of the fresh fruit. That is why they smell more of almond than of cherries. Pick Cherry sweets, then Marzipan, to compare the mixes. The main note stays the same. What changes is everything around it.

The model draws five of the key odorants. How much of each is in a mix is the model's own guess. It follows the order the studies give, with benzaldehyde first. None of these amounts is measured. The sweets and marzipan mixes are typical shapes, not real recipes. Only benzaldehyde is drawn atom by atom; the other four are simple shapes.

05

The flavorist's desk

A flavorist is a person who creates flavors for food. To make cherry, a flavorist does not copy the whole fruit. They pick the few molecules that matter most, far fewer than the fruit holds. Then they taste and adjust. Your turn: move the sliders to add drops of each molecule, and see what your mix smells like.

Where does benzaldehyde come from? One source is fruit kernels, the seeds inside the stones, such as bitter almonds and apricot kernels. Purified bitter almond oil is about 98 % benzaldehyde. Another source is cassia oil, from the bark of a tree related to cinnamon. In the US, benzaldehyde made from it is sold as natural. In the EU, it is not settled whether it counts as natural.

Most benzaldehyde, though, is made from toluene, a chemical from crude oil. Only about 1.5 % comes from plants. Benzaldehyde is the second most valuable flavor molecule, after vanillin, the smell of vanilla. The molecule is exactly the same whichever way it is made, and so is its smell. Only the label changes. In the EU, a “natural cherry flavoring” must come at least 95 % from cherries.

One word of care. Kernels do not hold benzaldehyde ready-made. When a kernel is crushed, it makes benzaldehyde and also cyanide, a poison. Even one small raw apricot kernel can be too much for a toddler. So never eat raw fruit kernels, and never crack them open to taste. The flavoring in ice cream is different: it is a purified ingredient, checked and approved for food.

06

Why ice cream needs more flavor

You can only smell molecules that get into the air. Aroma locked inside the bite does nothing. Cold keeps it locked in, so a frozen bite lets out very little. As the bite melts and warms in your mouth, more and more aroma escapes. In one study, vanilla ice cream tasted stronger served at −9 °C than at −14 °C.

Fat matters too. Cherry molecules are fat-loving: they dissolve in fat and like to stay there. So in a rich ice cream, the fat holds cherry back. In a low-fat one, cherry comes out faster and stronger. Vanilla works the other way round.

In the US, ice cream must have at least 10 % milk fat, and premium kinds have up to about 20 %. With the cold and the fat, much of the aroma stays locked in. So flavorists add more: cherry flavoring makes up about 1 to 2 % of the mix. Try the sliders. A warmer serving or less fat lets more aroma reach your nose.

Now put it all together. Your tongue tells you sweet and a little sour. Your nerves feel the cold and the cream. And your nose, which gets the aroma from behind your palate, tells you cherry. Pinch it, and the cherry is gone.

How strongly cold and fat hold aroma back is the model's own estimate. Studies show which way each one works, but not by how much. So read these numbers as a sketch, not a measurement.
