01

A speedboat with no one aboard

You are looking at a Magura V5, a naval drone from Ukraine: a fast boat with nobody on board. The state firm SpetsTechnoExport builds it for GUR, Ukraine's defence intelligence. It was first shown at the IDEF fair in Istanbul in July 2023. The boat is 5.5 m long and 1.5 m wide, about the size of a small speedboat. Its deck sits only 0.5 m above the water, and it weighs under one tonne. A pilot far away steers it over a satellite link.

The cutaway lets you look inside. The hull is composite: layers of fibre cloth set in hard resin. The bow holds a closed payload bay for up to 320 kg. Behind it come the fuel tanks, then the engine in an aluminium tub bedded in foam, with the electronics above it. The engine drives a waterjet at the stern. The maker has not named the engine, so the model assumes a jet-ski-type petrol engine, like the ones in the early boats. On deck sit two Starlink antennas, a backup satellite antenna, a low dome with a thermal camera and a fixed camera in the bow.

The model follows one run. The boat idles off a slipway, climbs onto the plane and cruises at 22 knots. A knot is one nautical mile an hour, so that is about 41 km/h. Then it speeds up to its top speed of 42 knots, about 78 km/h, and two more boats join it. The run ends as its bow reaches the side of a large grey ship at anchor.

At normal speed the boat moves as fast as a real one, but the trip is cut short to fit into two minutes. A real boat would cruise for many hours: about 20 hours to cover its full range of 450 nautical miles, about 800 km. Here the cruise lasts 42 seconds, and the sprint is shortened too. The ship lies just 1.5 km from the slipway, while a real target would be hundreds of kilometres away. 02

It stops floating and starts skimming

At rest the boat floats like any other. The water it pushes aside holds it up, and this is called buoyancy. As the boat speeds up, a second force joins in. Water striking the slanted bottom pushes up on it, and the faster the boat goes, the harder the push. Once this push carries most of the weight, the hull rises and skims over the water. That is planing. Even then, the back of the bottom always stays in the water.

Every boat that pushes through the water makes a bow wave. At about 5.7 knots, that wave is as long as this 5.5 m hull. This is the hull speed, a rule of thumb for boats that only float. It is not a speed limit: at 42 knots the Magura runs at about 7 times it. To get there, it has to climb the resistance hump, where the boat makes big waves and the drag, the force of the water holding it back, climbs steeply. The sharp edges along the bottom, the chines, add drag here too. They pay off only once the boat planes.

Move the slider, or tap a chip, and watch the bow. Near the hump it rises the most, then it settles as the boat planes. This angle is the trim. Drag is lowest at about 3 to 4 degrees, and the boat runs a little flatter at top speed. On this boat planing starts at about 15 to 18 knots, and the model's readout switches at 16. Then the wet part of the bottom shrinks fast, from about 4.5 m at 15 knots to about 1.5 to 2 m at 42 knots. No hump data is published for this boat, so the 6 degree peak and the split of the weight in the readouts are the model's own numbers.

The bottom is a deep V, which softens the blows from waves. Its angle is not published: the model gives it 20 degrees at the stern, growing sharper toward the bow. The square stern has a sharp lower edge, so at speed the water breaks away cleanly and the stern runs dry. Along each side, a thin spray rail throws spray outward; it is the model's guess. Lifting strakes, ridges lower down that help a hull rise onto the plane, are left off, because no photo shows them. Too much trim brings its own trouble: above a certain angle, a planing boat starts to bounce in a steady rhythm, which is called porpoising.

03

A pump instead of a propeller

At the stern sits a waterjet, a pump that does the job of a propeller. Water comes in through an opening in the bottom, behind a grate of steel bars. A spinning impeller, a small fan-like rotor, speeds it up. Fixed vanes behind it, the stator, take the swirl out of the flow. Then a nozzle narrows the stream and shoots it out behind the boat. The water flow view shows its path.

The push comes from speeding up the water that passes through the pump. The thrust equals the mass of water going through each second, times how much faster the jet leaves than the boat moves. So the jet must always be faster than the boat. In an ideal pump, the share of the work that moves the boat is 2 / (1 + jet speed / boat speed). When the jet leaves at one and a half times the boat's speed, that share is 80 %. At low speed the jet is far faster than the boat, and most of the work is lost.

Above about 25 to 30 knots, a jet matches or beats a propeller, and it has other strengths. Nothing hangs under the hull to add drag or hit things, so the boat can run in shallow water. The grate mostly clears itself at planing speed, but weed and rope can still block an intake. The first boats in 2022 used jet-ski parts: a three-cylinder Rotax petrol engine and a Sea-Doo pump. The Magura's own engine is not disclosed. The model's pump is jet-ski sized, 155 mm across, and at 42 knots it moves roughly 100 to 200 litres of seawater a second.

There is no rudder. To turn, the steering nozzle swings the stream up to 27 degrees to either side. Swing it right and the stern is pushed left, so the bow turns right, just as on a jet ski. To stop or back up, a reverse bucket drops over the nozzle and turns the stream forward and down. The slider sets the throttle, which the model takes as how much water the pump moves. 04

Steered from hundreds of kilometres away

With nobody on board, everything depends on the radio link. In November 2023 a Magura was captured in western Crimea, and Russian sources later showed what was inside. They found two Starlink satellite terminals, a backup satellite antenna made by Kymeta, a GPS receiver and a small computer running Linux. A mobile router with two SIM cards gave a link near the shore, within about 40 km. The maker also lists a mesh radio network, autonomous control and up to three video streams, but those are its own claims.

The pilot works at a console that travels in a hard case, with screens, levers and joysticks. Long trips across the sea can be programmed in advance, but a pilot watches the whole way, and the last stretch is often steered by hand. So the boat is not fully autonomous. Tap the chips to switch between Starlink, the backup satellite and no link at all. What a real boat does when its link drops is not published, so the model simply keeps it on its set course.

Starlink is quick for a satellite service. Its typical delay on land is about 25 to 35 milliseconds, and at sea it can be over 100. The tighter limit is the upload, 10 to 30 megabits a second, which is about what three video streams need. So the upload, not the delay, sets how sharp the pilot's picture is. Still, the picture always arrives a little late, and nobody has published how late. Drag the slider to try a delay: at 42 knots, a quarter of a second is 5.4 m, about one boat length. The ghost boat shows where the pilot sees it.

Finding the way needs care too. Since mid-2024, jammed and faked GPS signals over the Black Sea have been well documented, with ships showing up inland on tracking maps. That is why the maker also lists inertial and visual navigation, which work from the boat's own motion sensors and cameras. Starlink has a story here as well. In September 2022, Ukraine expected coverage near Crimea that had never been turned on. A 2023 book about Elon Musk first told it differently, and its author corrected the account after Musk said SpaceX did not deactivate anything. The model draws the satellites small, low and close, moving with the boat, so that you can see them.

How a command reaches the boat

  1. Pilot: moves a lever or a joystick on the console.
  2. Ground station: a Starlink station on land sends the command up by radio.
  3. Satellite: a Starlink satellite passes it down to the boat.
  4. Boat: an antenna on deck picks it up, and the computer moves the throttle or the steering nozzle.
  5. Video: the camera pictures travel back the same way and reach the pilot a little late.

05

Low enough to hide behind the curve of the sea

The sea curves away, so you can only see so far across it. From a height of h metres, the horizon lies about 3.86 × √h kilometres away. The boat's thermal camera sits about 0.7 m above the water, so its horizon is only about 3 km off. The 3D sea in the model is flat, because over 1.5 km the real curve drops only about 0.1 m. The drawing below makes the curve much steeper so that you can see it.

A ship's radar has the same problem. Its antenna is high, but the boat is only about 0.5 m tall. Radio waves bend a little around the Earth, so the radar's line of sight reaches about 4.12 × (√h1 + √h2) kilometres, with both heights in metres. For a radar 20 m up, that is about 21 km, and at 42 knots the boat covers it in about 16 minutes. But this is only the farthest the radar could ever see the boat, not where it actually spots it. Drag the slider to raise or lower the radar.

Real detection comes much later. A small boat sends back a weak echo. Its radar cross-section, the size of target it looks like to a radar, is about 0.5 to 5 m², and no figure has been published for the Magura. A standard for coastal radar expects a target of 1 m², 1 m above the sea, to be found at about 9 km, and only by an advanced radar in seas up to state 4. At 42 knots the boat covers that in about 7 minutes. Waves make it harder still: a small boat may be hidden behind them up to half the time, and the echoes from the waves themselves, called sea clutter, can drown out its own.

The people who run these boats say they are hard to spot. A commander of Group 13, GUR's naval drone unit, said radar has a hard time seeing its plastic hull and that the boat gives off little heat. These are claims, and no measurement has been published. One thing is on record: the engine sits in a foam-lined aluminium tub with the electronics above it, which keeps heat away from the outer skin. The boats are painted grey, and the best-known attacks came at night or at dawn. Tap the chips to change the sea state, a scale of wave height from smooth to rough.

06

Cheap, small and changing fast

A Magura V5 costs about a quarter of a million dollars. The maker quotes about USD 273,000, and other estimates say about USD 250,000. Sea Baby, a separate design by the SBU, Ukraine's security service, costs about USD 221,000. GUR values the patrol ship Sergey Kotov, which sank in March 2024 after a Magura attack, at about USD 65 million. These are public figures, shown side by side in the readouts below.

These boats rarely work alone. Reported attacks used groups of about five to ten, and the developers say each boat in a group has its own role. The ships that were reported sunk took several hits, not one. In the run, two companion boats join for the sprint; how they line up is the model's choice.

The design keeps changing. In December 2024, GUR fitted V5 boats with two R-73 air-to-air missiles on rails, renamed Sea Dragon. Tap the chips to see this fit. Only the deck changes: the model's boat runs the same route and still ends beside the ship. In 2025 GUR showed the larger Magura V7, 7.2 m long, with a payload of up to 650 kg. Sea Baby is the SBU's own line, not a Magura.

The record below keeps to what each side has said, and many of the claims are disputed. The UK Ministry of Defence counts at least 26 Russian navy ships damaged or destroyed between February 2022 and June 2024, about a third of the Black Sea Fleet. That count covers every kind of weapon, not only drone boats. By October 2023, satellite images showed about 14 ships moved from Sevastopol to Novorossiysk.
