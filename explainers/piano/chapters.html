01

You never touch the string

This is a concert grand, a Steinway model D. It is 274 cm long, weighs about 480 kg and has more than 12,000 parts. Under the open lid you can see the strings, the gold cast iron plate and, below them, the wooden soundboard. Its 88 keys play notes from 27.5 to 4,186 hertz (Hz), or vibrations per second. The model is cut open at middle C, so you can watch one key at work.

Press a key and it goes down about 10 mm, while its back end rises. That lifts the damper, a felt pad, off the strings. Then a felt hammer swings up 45 to 47 mm and strikes the strings from below. The note rings until you let the key up and the damper drops back onto the strings.

Here is the surprise: your finger never touches the string, because the key is a lever that throws the hammer. For the last millimetre or two the hammer flies on its own, so nothing is pushing it when it hits. At a medium touch it reaches the string about 67 milliseconds (ms) after your finger meets the key. A millisecond is a thousandth of a second. It drops to about 36 ms when you press hard and grows to about 160 ms when you press softly.

The playback is slowed down, and not evenly: the quickest moments are stretched the most so you can see them. The time readout on the gauge always shows the real clock. At the default playback speed, the ringing note plays at its real pace. Turn the sound on to hear it. It is not a recording: your browser synthesises the note from this model's numbers.

02

A lever that throws

The key is a seesaw. It rocks on a pin, so when you push the front down, the back end rises. There a screw called the capstan lifts the wippen, a hinged lever. The wippen carries the jack, an L-shaped piece that pushes up on the knuckle, a leather-covered roller under the hammer's shank.

The hammer swings up on a pivot, with its felt head about 130 mm out along the shank. Together, the levers multiply the key's movement. The hammer head travels about 5 to 6 times as far as the front of the key in the same time, so it also moves roughly that much faster. Turn on Follow the hammer to ride along with it.

At a medium touch the key reaches 0.3 to 0.5 metres per second (m/s), and the hammer about 1.5 to 2.5 m/s. A soft note sends the hammer up at about 1 m/s, a loud one at about 5 m/s, or 18 km/h. That loud hammer hits like something dropped from a tall person's shoulder. Pick a touch and watch the readouts change.

About 50 g resting on the front of a key is enough to start it moving. Playing takes more: about 8 newtons (N) for a soft note and up to 50 N for a loud one, the weight of about 5 kg. In this model every lever is perfectly stiff, and the key always lands just after the strike. A real action bends a little under a hard blow, and the order changes with loudness. In a soft note the hammer reaches the string first, while in a hard note the key lands about 3 ms before the hammer hits.

03

Letting go

Just before the hammer reaches the string, the jack has to get out of its way. Its tail runs into the let-off button, a small stop above it. The jack tips, and its top slips out from under the knuckle: this is the escapement. It happens 1 to 3 mm below the string, about the thickness of one or two euro coins. The let-off close-up shows this corner four times larger.

From there the hammer flies free. In this model the gap is 1.5 mm, so the flight lasts about 1.5 ms in a soft note and 0.3 ms in a loud one. At middle C the hammer then stays on the string for about 2 ms, as brief as a camera shutter at 1/500 of a second, and bounces off. Nothing you do after let-off can change the blow. Your touch only sets how fast the hammer is going when the jack lets go, and that speed decides how loud and how bright the note is.

After the strike the hammer falls back, and something has to catch it. The back check, a felt-covered block at the back of the key, holds it 13 to 16 mm below the string. A hard blow bounces back faster, so it is caught lower. As long as you hold the key down, the hammer waits there, clear of the string, and the note rings freely.

The repetition lever is what makes a grand quick. It springs up under the knuckle and holds the hammer partway up, so the jack can slip back under early. The key only has to rise about a third of the way before it can strike again. Makers say a grand key can repeat up to about 15 times a second, against about 7 on an upright. Bartolomeo Cristofori built the first escapement into his piano about 1700, and Érard patented this double escapement in 1821.

04

Dampers and pedals

Most notes have a damper, a felt pad that rests on the strings and keeps them silent. Small lead weights hold it down. When you press a key, its back end lifts the damper before the hammer arrives. In one measured loud note, the damper left the strings about 15 ms before the strike.

In this model the damper lets go after the first millimetre of key travel. That point is an assumption, because no source gives it for a grand. A slower touch takes longer over the rest of the stroke, so the damper is up even longer before the strike, as the readout shows. When you let the key up, the damper drops back and the note stops. The top 18 to 21 notes have no dampers at all: pick Treble C7 and the note rings on after you let go.

The sustain pedal, on the right, lifts every damper at once. The note keeps ringing after you let the key go, and other strings start to ring along with it. Measured on a Yamaha concert grand, middle C took 9.5 s to fade with the key held down, and 15.3 s with the pedal. Turn on String vibration to see the other strings shimmer.

The left pedal, called una corda, slides the keyboard and the whole action a little to the right. Each hammer then hits two of its three strings, or one of two in the bass. The string it misses still rings a little through the bridge, and the note comes out softer and duller. How far the action moves is not published, so the model shows the shift without a figure. On an upright the soft pedal works differently: it just moves the hammers closer to the strings.

05

From string to room

A string is too thin to push much air on its own. Instead it presses on the bridge, a long wooden rail glued to the soundboard. The soundboard is a wide sheet of spruce, 6 to 9 mm thick, and it works like a loudspeaker. The vibration passes through the bridge into the board, and the board moves the air. String vibration is on here, so you can watch both move, greatly exaggerated.

A concert grand has about 230 strings, each pulling with 700 to 900 newtons, about the weight of an adult. Together that makes about 20 tonnes of force, roughly what ten cars weigh. A wooden frame could not hold that, so a cast iron plate, 160 to 180 kg on a concert grand, takes the load together with the case. The wound bass strings are steel wrapped in copper, and they cross over the others. The copper adds weight, so the string sounds low without having to be very long.

A string vibrates at its main pitch and at a series of higher pitches at the same time, called partials. Steel wire is stiff, so its partials come out slightly sharp, above simple multiples of the main pitch. The readout counts this in cents, hundredths of the step from one key to the next. Tuners stretch their octaves a little wider to match. In the deep bass the soundboard can barely send out anything below about 100 Hz, so you hear those notes mostly through their partials.

A struck note fades in two stages: first a quick drop, the prompt sound, then a slow aftersound that can last many seconds. A faster hammer makes the note louder and brighter, because the felt gets harder as it squashes and brings out more high partials. The decay readout gives the time a note takes to fade by 60 decibels (dB). Those times were measured on a Yamaha concert grand, not this Steinway, and the C7 figures are this model's own estimates. On the gauge, the sound level counts dB below a hard strike in this model, not loudness in the room.

06

Upright and harpsichord

The line-up sets three actions side by side. On the grand, the hammer swings up from below and falls back by its own weight. The upright action is the same idea turned on its side. Its strings stand upright, so its hammer travels level toward them. Springs and a thin tape called the bridle pull it back.

Most uprights also have no repetition lever, so the jack resets only when the key has risen almost all the way. Makers say an upright key repeats about 7 times a second, against up to about 15 on a grand. In this model the upright uses the grand's key and hammer distances, so you can compare the two fairly. Turn on the ghost upright action in any chapter to see it next to the grand's.

The harpsichord makes its note another way. Its key lifts a wooden strip, the harpsichord jack, and a small plectrum on the jack plucks the string. However fast you press, the pluck stays the same, so your touch cannot make a note louder or softer. In Cristofori's day a harpsichord was louder and more brilliant than his piano, but it could not answer the player's touch. This model draws its jack in a simple form, not copied from a real instrument.

In 1711 the writer Scipione Maffei described Cristofori's instrument as a gravicembalo col piano, e forte, a harpsichord with soft and loud. Soft and loud, piano and forte in Italian, were exactly what a harpsichord's keys could not give. The clavichord could, but it was so quiet that you could barely hear it a few feet away.
