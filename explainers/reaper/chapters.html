01

Ten hours, and nobody on board

You are looking at an MQ-9 Reaper, a drone built by General Atomics in California and flown by the US Air Force since 2007. It is a big aircraft: the wing spans 20 m, and at takeoff it can weigh about 4,760 kg, almost as much as two and a half cars. Nobody sits inside. A crew in a ground station flies it, often from thousands of kilometres away. Its main job is to watch an area for hours, but it also carries weapons, so the crew that finds a target can strike it too. That is why it has been called a hunter-killer.

The model follows one armed mission of about ten and a half hours, from the takeoff roll to the stop after landing. A crew at the airfield takes off and starts the climb. Half an hour later, a second crew far away takes over through a satellite. The Reaper then circles one area for six and a half hours, fires one Hellfire missile and flies home, where the airfield crew lands it.

To fit all of this on your screen, the model takes a few shortcuts. It shrinks every distance, so the whole flight fits into a few kilometres of desert, and it flies two and a half laps over the area where a real Reaper would fly many more. The aircraft is drawn twenty times too big for the ground around it, or you could not see it from 7.6 km up. The length of the mission, the time of each handover and the fuel used along the way are the model's own choices, not published figures.

The clock at the top counts the time since the takeoff roll began, so T+1:30:00 means an hour and a half into the mission. The whole flight plays in about a minute and a half, and you can drag the timeline to any moment or tap a chip below to jump there. Watch the readouts as you go: the radio link changes when the crews swap, and the fuel drops hour by hour. The radio links switch shows the beams, and the flight track switch shows the path flown so far.

02

Built to stay up, not to go fast

Start with the wing. It is long and narrow, a slender shape you might expect on a glider. Engineers describe this with the aspect ratio, which compares a wing's span with its width. The Reaper's is about 17, more than twice the 7.5 of a small Cessna 172. A long, narrow wing wastes less energy holding the aircraft up, and that pays off when you want to stay in the air for many hours.

But the Reaper is no glider. Each square metre of its wing carries about 200 kg, almost three times as much as on a Cessna. So it has to fly fairly fast to stay up, and for that it needs a strong engine: a turboprop, a small gas turbine that turns a propeller through a gearbox. The Honeywell engine gives 900 horsepower, and its three-blade propeller sits at the tail and pushes the aircraft along.

Why a propeller and not a jet? A propeller gives a large mass of air a small push, which is the thrifty choice at low speed. And the Reaper is in no hurry: it cruises at about 370 km/h and circles over an area at about 300 km/h. The turbine brings its own advantage. It keeps its power in thin, high air, so the Reaper can climb far higher than the older Predator, whose piston engine held it below about 7.6 km.

About 38 % of the Reaper's takeoff weight is fuel, roughly 1,800 kg, and that is the real secret behind its long flights. The cutaway opens the body so you can see the engine and the fuel tank inside. At the back, two fins point up in a V and a small fin points down, so from behind the tail looks like a Y. The Predator's V pointed down instead, and the Reaper is a much bigger machine: about 4.7 times as heavy, with almost eight times the power. Tap the chips to set it beside the Predator or a Cessna 172.

03

Flown from far away, through space

With nobody on board, everything depends on the radio links. Near the airfield the Reaper uses a direct line-of-sight link. It runs in the C-band, one range of radio frequencies, and the antenna on the ground needs a clear view of the aircraft. Farther away, the curve of the Earth gets in the way. So for the mission the Reaper switches to a satellite link in the Ku-band, another range of frequencies, through a dish inside the hump on top of its nose.

Two crews share the work. A small launch and recovery crew at the airfield handles the takeoff and the landing over the direct link. The mission crew flies everything in between from a ground station in the United States, for example at Creech Air Force Base in Nevada. In the model the handover comes half an hour after takeoff, and the airfield crew takes the aircraft back at T+10:15:00. This classic split is still common. Yet the Reaper has been able to land automatically since 2018, and the Air Force says landings under satellite control are becoming standard.

A command from Nevada takes a long road. It runs through fibre-optic cables to a relay station, which sends it up to a satellite by radio. A German court traced one such route: for flights over Yemen, the data went by cable to Ramstein Air Base in Germany and up from there. The model draws the satellite close by, but the real one sits 35,786 km above the equator. One trip up and back down is about 1.8 times around the Earth, and even at the speed of light it takes about a quarter of a second.

A command going up and the video coming back make two such trips, so about half a second. With the cables and the equipment along the way, the pilot sees the result of a move about half a second to two seconds later. That is why the crew flies much of the mission through the autopilot. If the link drops, the aircraft follows a route set in advance toward a place where it can be recovered.

04

A camera ball that sees in the dark

Under the nose hangs the sensor ball. It is about 56 cm across, and its sensor module weighs about 104 kg. The ball can turn and tilt, so the crew can keep its cameras on one spot while the aircraft circles around it. On the way out it looks ahead and down. Over the area it stays on the target, as the readout below shows.

Inside the ball sit several eyes. Daylight cameras film in colour and in black and white. An infrared camera sees heat instead of light, so warm engines and people stand out even at night. Next to them are three lasers: a rangefinder that measures distance, an illuminator that lights up a scene, and a designator. Tap the chips to switch between the daylight view, the infrared view and the laser.

The laser that matters most for a strike is the designator, which marks a target for a guided weapon. Its light has a wavelength of 1,064 nm, in the infrared, so no eye can see it: the bright line in the model is drawn only so you can follow it. The light comes in short pulses that follow a code. The weapon is set to the same code, so it homes only on its own spot.

Cameras cannot see through cloud, but radar can. The Lynx radar, built into the aircraft, makes detailed pictures of the ground through cloud and rain and picks out things that move. In the ground station, the pilot flies and the sensor operator runs the ball and the lasers. Often a third crew member, an intelligence coordinator, helps them make sense of what they see. On a long mission like this one, fresh crews take over the seats several times.

05

A missile that follows an invisible spot

The weapons hang on pylons, the mounts under the wings. The model carries a classic load: four Hellfire missiles on two-rail launchers, and two GBU-12 bombs closer to the body, where the pylons can hold the most weight. A Hellfire is about 1.6 m long and weighs about 45 kg. Since a software update in 2020, a Reaper can carry up to eight of them.

A Hellfire can reach a target about 8 km away, pushed by a solid rocket motor. It finds its way by semi-active laser homing, which means it carries no laser of its own. A seeker in its nose looks for laser light bouncing off the target. That light comes from the designator, which the sensor operator keeps on the spot. The seeker can lock on to the spot before launch, or find it after.

So the crew cannot fire and look away. The laser has to stay on the target until the missile hits, and a typical flight takes about 20 to 30 seconds. Drag the slider to move the target closer, and watch both the flight time and the time the laser must stay on get shorter. The model shows the time as a range, because sources disagree on how fast a Hellfire flies.

The Reaper can also drop bombs. The GBU-12 steers onto a laser spot as well, while the GBU-38, also called JDAM, finds its way with GPS. In the model the target is a made-up compound, and the strike takes only seconds of the long mission. The timeline stretches those seconds out so you can follow them. Tap the chips to jump to the launch or the impact.

06

Up to a day in the air

Time in the air is the Reaper's real strength. Without weapons it can stay up for about 27 hours, longer than the longest airline flight. Fully armed, that drops to about 14 hours. The Reaper cannot take a full tank and a full load of weapons at once: together they would weigh more than it is allowed to take off with, so the crew trades fuel for weapons. Tap the chips to compare an unarmed and an armed Reaper.

On a mission it usually flies at about 7.6 km, around 25,000 ft. It could climb higher, up to about 15 km, although sources disagree on its exact ceiling. Over the area it circles slowly with the engine at low power, and that is what makes the fuel last. The armed mission in the model, about ten and a half hours, fits well inside those 14 hours.

Distance eats into those hours from both ends. Every hour spent flying out to the area, and every hour flying back, is an hour not spent watching. Drag the slider to move the area farther away. The model has the Reaper cruise at about 370 km/h each way and keep one hour of fuel in reserve, then shows how much time is left over the area.

One aircraft cannot watch a spot around the clock. Keeping one patrol going without a break, which the Air Force calls a combat line, typically takes four aircraft and about 200 people. Crews take turns in the ground station, and aircraft take turns over the area. The fleet is shrinking, though: the US Air Force had 351 Reapers in 2022 and 135 in May 2026, and the production line has closed.
