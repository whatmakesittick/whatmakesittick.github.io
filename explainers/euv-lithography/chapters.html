01

A machine that makes its own light

This machine prints the finest layers of the most advanced computer chips. It is very heavy. The newest model, called High-NA, weighs more than 150 tonnes and shipped in more than 250 crates, 43 containers and 20 trucks. (NA stands for numerical aperture, how wide a cone of light the last mirror collects. The last chapter explains why it matters.) No maker publishes how long or tall the machine is, so every size and position in this model is its own.

The machine prints with extreme ultraviolet light, or EUV. You cannot see it. Its wavelength is only 13.5 nm, and a nanometre (nm) is a millionth of a millimetre. Air and glass swallow this light: 1 mm of air lets through about 0.14 percent, and 1 µm of glass about 0.004 percent. So nothing in this machine is a lens. Every part that steers the light is a mirror, and the light travels in a near-vacuum with a little hydrogen.

The machine makes its own light, one flash at a time, from a tiny drop of tin. A drive laser sits on the floor below. Its beam climbs through a long beam path, up to 30 m, to the source vessel. The laser delivers tens of kilowatts and weighs more than 10 tonnes.

The wafer is a thin round slice of silicon. It goes in, the machine prints it one patch at a time, and it comes out. This page follows one flash instead, from the tin drop to the wafer. The model is a simplification. Nobody can see a beam in a vacuum, and EUV is not visible at all, so the beam colours are drawn by us. The clock is the model's own too. A drop flies for about 20 ms, but the flash lasts about 20 ns, so playback stretches and squeezes time.

02

A droplet, hit twice

Every flash starts with a drop of tin about 30 µm wide. That is a bit more than half the width of a human hair, which is about 50 µm (a rounded figure). The droplets are fired, not dropped, at 60 to 120 metres a second. About 50,000 of them go by each second, roughly 1.2 to 2.4 mm apart. The real machine does not publish which way they fly, so the model chooses a direction.

A laser hits each drop twice. The first, gentler pulse flattens it into a thin pancake more than 100 µm wide. A flat target takes the next pulse much better than a ball. The second pulse is the big one: a CO2 laser with light of 10.6 µm, boosted more than 10,000 times from a small seed pulse. Two pulses for each drop make about 100,000 laser pulses a second.

The big pulse heats the tin to about 220,000 °C, 30 to 40 times hotter than the surface of the Sun. The tin turns into plasma, a gas so hot that its atoms lose electrons and glow. Tin ions in the plasma glow at 13.5 nm, which is why this machine uses tin, and small drops leave little debris. Only about 5 percent of the laser light becomes EUV, and that figure is from the lab.

The collector mirror, a big curved mirror, catches the light and gathers it. The vessel holds low-pressure hydrogen that stops tin specks and cleans tin off the mirror. A collector has been shown to last more than 100 billion pulses.

03

Light that only bounces

EUV mirrors are not shiny metal. Each one is a multilayer mirror: more than 100 thin layers of silicon and molybdenum, each a few nm thick. Every boundary between layers reflects a little light. The layers sit about 6.8 to 6.9 nm apart, half the wavelength, so all the small reflections add up together.

Even so, a mirror gives back only about 70 percent of the light. That is the best measured, not a promise. So the light loses about a third at every bounce. The model counts 11 bounces after the intermediate focus: four illuminator mirrors, the mask, and six projection mirrors. About 2 percent of the light reaches the wafer, and the range is 1 to 4 percent. The count rests on one paper, and another counts 10.

These mirrors must be extremely smooth. Their roughness is about 50 pm, and a picometre (pm) is a thousandth of a nanometre. Zeiss, the company that makes them, says that on a mirror as wide as Germany the highest bump would be about 0.1 mm.

The collector gathers the light at one point, the intermediate focus, and the beam spreads again from there. Use the slider to follow the beam bounce by bounce, and watch how much light is left. The model draws the folds and positions of the mirrors itself, following published diagrams.

04

A mirror with a map on it

The mask, also called the reticle, holds the pattern of one chip layer. It is a mirror too. A thin layer on top, the absorber, soaks up the light wherever the pattern has a line, so the light that bounces off carries the pattern. The mask sits on a square plate, 152 mm wide (six inches), and the pattern area is 104 by 132 mm.

Before the mask comes the illuminator, four mirrors that shape the beam. Their surfaces are made of many small tiles, called facets, that can be tilted. The tilts set the angles at which light reaches the mask. One study measured 22 such settings on a single illuminator, an approximate figure.

The beam meets the mask at a slant of 6 degrees (5.4 on High-NA). The light going in and the light coming out are therefore 12 degrees apart, and the two beams do not collide. In this model the mask faces down. That placement is the model's own.

A pellicle covers the mask. It is a very thin film that keeps dust off, because a speck on the mask would print on every wafer. The light crosses it twice, in and out. More than 90 percent passes each time, and about 81 percent passes both times. A pellicle is a film, not a lens. After the mask, the projection mirrors shrink the pattern four times (8 times in one direction on High-NA).

05

A field at a time

A wafer holds many copies of a chip, and the machine prints one patch at a time. Each patch is a field, 26 by 33 mm. A wafer holds about 76 to 80 full fields. The light does not cover a whole field at once. It passes through a curved slit, about 26 by 2 mm.

To cover the field, the mask and the wafer move together in opposite directions. The slit sweeps over the mask, and the shrunk pattern sweeps over the wafer. This is called scanning. A machine can scan 160 wafers an hour (NXE:3600D) or up to 220 (NXE:3800E). Those are the makers' specs, for a dose of 30 mJ/cm2.

That dose is about 20 EUV photons on every square nm of the wafer. A photon is one particle of light. The moves must be very exact. Position sensors read about 60 pm, 20,000 times a second. Overlay is how well one layer lines up with the one below it, and it is 1.1 nm on the 3600D and 0.9 nm on the 3800E and 1.1 nm on the High-NA EXE:5000, from machine to machine.

The last stretch of the playback is the time one field takes to scan. In this model that is about 0.29 s at 160 wafers an hour and 0.21 s at 220. These times are approximate. The wafer time also includes loading and aligning, so a real exposure is shorter. Pick a machine to see the numbers change.

06

193 nm against 13.5 nm

Older machines use deep ultraviolet light, or DUV, at 193 nm. They use lenses, and a layer of water under the last lens. They print lines with a half pitch of about 36 to 38 nm. A half pitch is half the distance from one line to the next. EUV prints 13 nm lines, 13 nm apart, so the pitch is 26 nm.

EUV light has a much shorter wavelength, and its photon carries 91.8 eV of energy, about 14 times a DUV photon. The smallest line follows a rule: a number k1 times the wavelength, divided by the NA. NA is the numerical aperture, the width of the cone of light the last mirror collects. A wider cone sees finer detail. At a k1 of 0.3, the rule gives 12.3 nm at an NA of 0.33 and 7.4 nm at 0.55.

The High-NA machine has an NA of 0.55. It shrinks the pattern 4 times in one direction and 8 times in the other, so one field is half as big, 26 by 16.5 mm. It prints 8 nm half pitch. This model keeps the same mirror path for High-NA and changes only the last mirrors, the shrink, the field and the numbers, because the real mirror count is not known. The projection optics weigh about 12 tonnes and the illumination optics about 6.

To feel the scale, a flu virus is about 100 nm wide. About 8 of these lines fit across it. A hair is wide enough for thousands. The "3 nm" in chip names is only a name. The real spacing of its finest wires is about 23 to 24 nm. As for price, there is no official figure. A machine is reported to cost well over 100 million euros, and a High-NA one about 350 million.
