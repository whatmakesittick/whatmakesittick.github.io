01

A tunnel that listens to your body

This is an MRI scanner with a 1.5 tesla magnet. The tesla measures how strong a magnetic field is. You lie on the table, and it slides you into the bore, a tunnel about 70 cm wide. The machine around it is about 1.6 to 1.9 m long and weighs 3 to 6 tonnes.

For a brain scan, a head coil sits around your head like a cage. It is an antenna with 20 to 64 small receivers that listen for a faint radio echo from your body. The room is lined with copper sheet to keep outside radio out, so it cannot drown that echo. There are no X-rays here. A radio wave at this pitch carries about 50 million times too little energy to knock an electron out of an atom.

Under the white cover, layers sit one inside the other like rings: the magnet in its cold jacket, three gradient coils and a radio antenna called the body coil. Switch on the cutaway (C) to see them. The next chapters take them one at a time.

The model follows one repetition, the short cycle a scan runs again and again to build one picture. In this model one repetition lasts 0.5 to 2.5 s of real time, but the radio pulses inside it take only milliseconds. So the timeline is stretched: the pulses fill most of the loop and the long rest is squeezed. The timeline names the step, and the live readings show the real time. A whole head exam, with many pictures, takes about 30 to 45 minutes.

02

A magnet that never switches off

The biggest part is the main magnet: about 30 to 40 km of niobium-titanium wire wound into coils. Cooled far enough, this wire becomes superconducting, which means current flows through it with no resistance at all. Several hundred amperes go round and round in a closed loop. Once the loop is closed, holding the field takes no power. It fades by less than a tenth of a percent in a year.

To stay superconducting, the coils sit in liquid helium at 4.2 K, about −269 °C. That is only about 1.5 K warmer than deep space. A classic magnet holds about 1,500 litres of it, while newer sealed magnets need just 7. A heat shield and a vacuum around the helium vessel work like a thermos flask. A small fridge on top, the cold head, turns boiled helium back into liquid; it runs day and night and draws about 6 to 7 kW.

The field is tens of thousands of times stronger than Earth's, which changes from place to place. Pick 3 T to compare a stronger magnet. Extra shield coils on the outside push current the other way and pull the stray field in close. Even so, the 0.5 mT line, inside which a pacemaker may no longer be safe, reaches about 4 m along the tunnel. Turn on the field lines to see it.

Since the magnet never switches off, a lit sign at the door says so. Anything with iron in it can fly into the tunnel, and oxygen tanks have killed people this way. That is why staff check everyone and everything at the door. If the wire warms up somewhere, it stops superconducting and the current turns into heat: a quench. The field drops to zero in 2 to 5 seconds, and the boiling helium roars out of the building through the quench pipe.

03

Hydrogen leans and wobbles

About six in ten atoms in your body are hydrogen, mostly in water and fat. The core of a hydrogen atom, its nucleus, is a single proton. It behaves like a tiny magnet. MRI listens to hydrogen because it is everywhere and gives the strongest signal.

In the field, each tiny magnet does not simply point along it. It precesses: it wobbles around the field direction like a tilted spinning top. At 1.5 T it wobbles about 64 million times a second, 63.9 MHz. This rate is the Larmor frequency, and it grows with the field, to 127.7 MHz at 3 T. Both are close to FM radio, one just below the band and one just above it.

The nuclei do not all line up. Body heat jostles them, so they point almost every which way. Only about 5 in a million more lean with the field than against it. That small surplus still adds up to about 3 × 10¹⁴ nuclei in one cubic millimetre of water. Together they make a tiny net magnet, the arrow the scanner works with.

The arrows in the inset are a drawing, enlarged many times, not a real spot in the head. They also show the lean much stronger than it is, so you can see it. The drawn wobble turns half a turn per second, about 128 million times slower than the real one at 1.5 T. Switch to 3 T and watch the frequency and the surplus double.

04

A radio pulse and its echo

To make the net magnet speak, the scanner sends a short radio pulse at exactly the Larmor frequency. Matching the wobble like this is called resonance, like pushing a child on a swing at just the right moment. The pulse comes from the body coil, a cage of copper rungs just inside the tunnel. It lasts a few milliseconds at most.

A 90° pulse tips the net magnet over until it lies sideways. There it keeps wobbling, and as it sweeps past the head coil it makes a tiny voltage of a few microvolts. That is the signal. Move the tip angle slider: only the sideways part makes signal, the part along the field makes none.

The spins soon drift out of step, because some wobble a little faster than others, and the signal fades. Halfway to the echo, a 180° pulse flips them over, so the fast ones end up behind and catch up. They come back into step and give an echo at the echo time. Use the chips to stop at each moment.

Two clocks decide how bright each tissue looks. T1 is the time the net magnet takes to grow about 63 % of the way back along the field. T2 is the time the sideways signal takes to fade to about 37 % as the spins fall out of step. Fat recovers fast and brain fluid slowly; pick a tissue to compare. The pulses also warm you slightly, so the scanner keeps that heating within safe limits.

05

Coils that tell places apart

So far every spot in the head answers at the same pitch, so the echo cannot tell where it came from. Three more coils inside the magnet fix that: the gradient coils x, y and z. A gradient is a field that changes steadily from one side to the other. Each coil adds a little to the main field on one side of the centre and takes a little away on the other.

The change is small. At up to 45 mT per metre, the field at the edge of the head shifts by about 5.4 mT, a third of a percent of the main field. That is enough to give every position its own frequency. The coils switch on in about 0.2 ms.

Each coil has its own job. The z coil works with the radio pulse to wake just one thin slice: slice selection. The y coil gives each repetition a different twist from top to bottom: phase encoding. The x coil runs while the echo is heard, so its pitch tells left from right: frequency encoding. Pick a coil to see its field, or follow the scan.

Switching big currents inside a strong magnet makes the coils jolt, like a loudspeaker. That is the loud knocking you hear during a scan. It can pass 100 decibels, about as loud as a jackhammer or louder. That is why you always get earplugs or headphones.

06

From echoes to a picture

Each echo is stored as one line of k-space, a grid that sorts the echoes by pitch and twist instead of by place. One repetition fills one line; the next one changes the twist and fills another. In this model the picture is 64 × 64, so it takes 64 repetitions; real head scans often use 256 lines. Here the lines fill from the middle outwards, so a rough picture shows up early.

A maths step called the Fourier transform turns that grid into the picture. It finds which mix of stripes, broad and fine, adds up to the image. The middle of k-space holds the broad stripes, the overall light and shade. The edges hold the fine detail. Try 8, 16, 32 or 64 lines and watch the picture sharpen.

The timing decides which tissues look bright. A T1-weighted scan repeats every 500 ms and listens early, at 15 ms. The repetition ends before slow tissues have grown back, so fat is bright and brain fluid is dark. A T2-weighted scan waits 2,500 ms and listens at 100 ms: slow-fading fluid now shines, and grey matter beats white. In this model every tissue holds the same amount of hydrogen, and bone and air stay black.

With one line per repetition, 256 lines take about 2.1 minutes for the T1 scan and 10.7 for the T2 scan. Real scanners use turbo spin echo, which reads 4 to 32 lines in each repetition; at 16 lines, the T2 scan takes about 40 seconds. The idea is young. Paul Lauterbur published the first MR image in 1973, and he and Peter Mansfield shared the 2003 Nobel Prize for it.
