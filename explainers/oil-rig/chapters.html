01

A hole four kilometres deep, drilled from a ship that floats

You are looking at an offshore drilling rig, far out at sea. It does not stand on the seabed. It is a semi-submersible, a platform that floats on huge hollow hulls under the water. Here the sea is 1,000 m deep, and the rig is drilling a well that ends 4,500 m below its drill floor, deep inside the rock. Rigs like this one search for oil and gas under the sea. About a quarter of the world's oil now comes from offshore fields.

The rig is a floating drilling machine. A steel tower, the derrick, rises about 60 m above the drill floor. It holds a string of steel pipe with a drill bit at the end and turns it. The pipe runs down through the sea and into the rock. A heavy fluid called drilling mud is pumped down the pipe to keep the hole under control. As the hole gets deeper, the crew lines it with steel pipe and cement, section by section. A rig like this has beds for 130 to 200 people, and the work goes on day and night.

The loop follows the drill bit from the drill floor to the bottom of the well. The timeline counts metres below the drill floor, the way drillers measure depth. The floor sits 25 m above the sea, so the bit meets the seabed at 1,025 m. Below the sea the rock is cut open like a slice of cake, showing the layers and the well. The drawing squeezes the sea and the rock so the whole well fits on the screen, and it draws the pipes eight times wider than life so you can see them. So the picture is not to scale, but the ruler on the cut face is true.

Drag the model to look around it and scroll over it to zoom. As you read on, the view follows the text. The readouts show what the bit meets on its way: how far below the seabed it is, the mud pressure and the temperature around it, and how many days the well has taken so far. 02

Why it does not tip over

The camera is now at the waterline. The rig floats on two long hollow hulls called pontoons, each 110 m long, hidden under the water. Four square columns rise from them, pierce the surface and hold up the deck, a steel box 78 m across. On top sit the derrick, the cranes, the living quarters and a helideck. In the middle of the deck is the moonpool, an opening where the pipe goes down into the sea. Mooring lines and thrusters keep the rig in place.

It floats for the same reason a boat does. A floating body sinks until the water it pushes aside weighs as much as it does. While drilling, this rig pushes aside about 51,000 tonnes of seawater. To choose how deep it floats, the crew pumps seawater into tanks in the pontoons, or pumps it out. Moving between jobs, the rig rides high, about 9.5 m deep. For drilling it is ballasted down to about 21 m, so the pontoons sit deep and only the slim columns cross the surface. Try the slider.

Waves move the water in circles that shrink quickly with depth. Half a wavelength down, only about 4 percent of the motion is left. But ocean swells are long, and the pontoons are not that deep. In a long swell the water around them still moves almost half as much as at the surface. So depth does only half the job, and the slim columns do the other half. A passing wave hardly changes how much of the rig is under water, so it barely lifts it. Standing far apart at the corners, the columns also keep the rig from tipping. When it leans, the columns on the low side sink deeper and push it back up.

Floating also means drifting, so the rig has to be held over the well. Eight lines of chain and wire run from the columns to anchors on the seabed, which works in water up to about 1,500 m deep. Thrusters under the pontoons can hold it too, steered by a computer that follows satellites and beacons on the seabed. Other depths call for other rigs. A jack-up stands on legs on the seabed, in up to 150 m of water. A steel jacket has reached 412 m, a tension-leg platform 1,584 m and a spar about 2,400 m. The deepest water ever drilled, 3,628 m, was reached from a drillship. Try the depth slider.

03

Turning a bit from four kilometres away

The camera now rides beside the drill bit on its way down. Everything above the bit hangs from the derrick. Up in the derrick, the top drive, a set of powerful motors hanging from the hook, grips the pipe and turns it, and the whole string turns with it. The pipe comes in joints about 9.4 m long, screwed together in threes into stands of about 28 m. Each time a stand has been drilled down, the top drive lets go, climbs back up and picks up the next one. Zoom out to see it climb back every 28 m.

A well is drilled in sections, each narrower than the one before, like a telescope. The first hole is 36 in (914 mm) across and goes only 75 m into the seabed. The conductor, a steel pipe 30 in across, is lowered into it and cemented in place. Holes of 26, 17½ and 12¼ in follow, each lined with steel casing and cement before the next begins. The casing holds the hole open and seals off the rock behind it. Each string must fit through the one before, so the last hole, 8½ in, is only 216 mm across. Try the chips to jump to each section.

The rig does not push the pipe down. The string hangs from the hook, pulled straight by its own weight, because a pipe kilometres long and only 140 mm across would bend if it were pushed. The driller lowers it until only part of the weight of the heavy collars rests on the bit, about 3 to 20 tonnes. The top drive turns it at about 80 to 140 turns a minute. A roller-cone bit crushes the rock under toothed cones that roll around the bottom of the hole. A fixed-cutter bit has no moving parts: discs of man-made diamond on its blades shave the rock away. Fixed-cutter bits now drill more than 90 percent of all the metres drilled in the world.

Drilling is slow work. The bit cuts tens of metres an hour in soft rock, but only a few metres an hour in hard rock. When a bit wears out, the whole string has to come out, stand by stand, and go back in with a new bit. From 3,000 m that round trip takes about half a day. Running and cementing each casing string adds about two days more. Watch the day counter as the bit goes down, and switch bits to see how the time changes. In the model the well reaches total depth around day 30. Real wells in water this deep usually take one to three months.

04

Mud does three jobs

The camera is now at the seabed, 1,000 m down, where it is dark and about 4 °C. This is where the well begins. On top of the wellhead sits the blowout preventer, a stack of valves about 17 m tall. Above it the riser, a steel pipe 533 mm wide, climbs all the way to the rig. The drill pipe runs down the middle. The moving dots are the drilling mud. It goes down inside the pipe, out through the bit and back up the gap around the pipe, called the annulus, to the rig.

Drilling mud is a thick fluid made heavy with barite, a dense mineral ground to powder. Its first job is to hold back the rock. Deep down, the tiny spaces in the rock hold water, oil or gas under great pressure, and the column of mud in the well presses back. Its second job is to carry the cuttings, the chips of rock cut by the bit, up to the rig, where shaking screens sieve them out. Its third is to cool and clean the bit. At first the mud follows the drilling plan and gets heavier with each section. The slider lets you make it lighter or heavier.

The mud has to stay inside a window. Its pressure in bar is its density in g/cm³ times 0.0981 times the depth in metres. If that is less than the pore pressure, the pressure of the fluid in the rock, then gas, oil or water pushes into the well: a kick. If it is more than the fracture pressure, the rock cracks and swallows the mud. Under 1,000 m of water the window is narrow. Seawater weighs less than half as much as rock, so the rock below the seabed carries less weight and cracks more easily. Near the seabed the window almost closes. So the first two sections are drilled without a riser, with seawater or a light mud that spills out onto the seabed with the cuttings.

If a kick does get in, the blowout preventer can close the well. Its rams shut in under a minute and hold up to 1,034 bar. It is a last line of defence, not a guarantee: its cutting rams cannot cut through the thick joints between pipes. At the reservoir, the model's window runs from about 1.24 to 1.57 g/cm³, and the plan uses 1.40. Move the slider and watch the status and the chart. Too light, and gas bubbles rise in the return flow. Too heavy, and cracks spread out from the hole.

05

Why the oil is there

Now look at the cut face of the rock. The upper layers lie flat, but deep down two layers bend up into an arch under the rig. The top of the arch is the seal, a thick layer of shale that lets almost nothing through. Under it lies the reservoir, a sandstone that holds gas at the top, oil in the middle and water below. Far beneath, at the bottom of the block, lies the dark shale where the oil began.

The story starts with plankton, tiny living things that drifted in the sea millions of years ago. When they died, some sank into mud with so little oxygen that they did not rot away. Buried ever deeper, that mud became a dark shale full of their remains, the source rock. There heat did the slow cooking: between about 60 and 120 °C, over millions of years, the remains turned into oil. Oil is lighter than water, so it crept upward through the water-filled pores of the rock above until a tight layer stopped it. Try the chips to visit each layer.

A trap is an arch of porous rock under a seal, like a bowl turned upside down. Oil and gas gather in its crest and settle by weight: gas on top, then oil, then water. Here the oil ends at the oil–water contact, 4,220 m below the drill floor. The oil is not in a cave or a lake. It fills the tiny spaces between sand grains, about 22 percent of this sandstone. Down here the rock is about 96 °C, and the fluid in its pores is at about 505 bar. That is more than normal for this depth. As the thick shale was buried, the water could not escape, so it carries part of the weight of the rock above.

Nobody can see this far down. Survey ships send sound into the seabed and record the echoes. These map the shapes of the layers and show where a trap might be, but they cannot prove what fills the pores. Only a well can prove that oil is there. In 2024 only about 1 in 4 of the big exploration wells found oil or gas worth producing. Try the chips and watch the close-up of the grains. Gas, oil and water fill the same spaces in the sandstone, while the seal has almost none.

06

First oil

The bit has reached total depth, and the view now shows the well finished. The drill string is gone. Steel casing lines the hole all the way down, cemented in place. Inside it hangs the production tubing, a narrower pipe that carries the oil up to the rig. Down in the oil, guns lowered into the well have fired charges through the casing and the cement into the rock. These holes, the perforations, let the oil in.

Why does the oil rise on its own? The fluid in the reservoir is under pressure, here about 505 bar. That is more than a column of salty water reaching up to the surface would weigh. Oil is lighter than water, about 0.85 g/cm³, so a column of oil from the reservoir up to the rig weighs even less, about 346 bar. The difference pushes the oil out at the top with pressure to spare. First the well is tested. Oil and gas flow up to the rig, where the crew measures how much comes out. The flare boom burns what comes up, because a drilling rig has no pipeline to send it to. The flame at the tip of the boom marks the test. If the well is worth producing, it can later be fitted with a tree of valves on the seabed and linked by pipelines to a production platform, or to a ship that stores the oil in its hull until tankers take it away.

As oil comes out, the pressure in the reservoir falls. The model keeps this simple: the pressure drops by 1.4 percent of its starting value every year. Real fields vary a lot. When too little pressure is left at the top, the oil stops flowing on its own. Then a pump in the well, or gas lift, which pumps gas into the tubing to make the column lighter, keeps it coming. Even so, a field gives up only about a third of its oil on average. Move the slider and watch what is left at the top shrink year by year.
