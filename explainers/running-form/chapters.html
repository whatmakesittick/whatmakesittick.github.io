01

The stride

Watch the runner on the coastal road at sunrise. His run is a string of one-legged hops. One leg lands, gives a little and springs him back up. Then both feet leave the road for a moment. That moment in the air, the float, is what makes a run a run. In walking, one foot is always down. People switch from walking to running at about 2 m/s.

One stride is both steps: the right foot lands, then the left, then the right again. A step runs from one foot landing to the other. Follow the right leg through four phases. In landing, the foot touches down and the leg gives. In push-off, the leg pushes until the foot leaves the road. In recovery, the heel lifts and the knee folds and swings forward. In reach, the leg swings out and comes back down. The left leg does the same half a stride later. Drag the timeline to see it.

The model draws the borders between the phases itself. Stance is the time your foot is on the road. Mid-stance is where the road's push turns from braking to pushing you forward. Mid-swing is where the swinging knee is bent the most. The coloured bands on the timeline are for the steady pace. At the other paces the real borders move a little.

Your feet are on the road for less than half of each stride, and less as you speed up. The rest is the two floats, one after each foot leaves. In this data both floats together take about 19 to 32 percent of the stride, from the easy pace to the fast one. Other labs count less, because they measure it another way. Your body is lowest near mid-stance and highest in the middle of a float. In walking it is the reverse: you are highest in the middle of a step.

Speed is simple arithmetic: step rate, the steps you take in a minute, times step length, the distance of one step. Step rate is also called cadence. The three paces are the model's own names, not race paces. They are three treadmill speeds from a public lab dataset, shown per kilometre in the dock. Pick a pace and watch both numbers grow.

Where do the curves come from? The angles of the hip, knee and ankle are the averages of 31 to 39 competitive runners on a treadmill, all in the same lab shoes. The curves were averaged and refitted for display, so a real runner differs. The data has nothing for the upper body, so the model moves it by rules taken from other studies. The road, the sea and the sun are a design, not a place.

02

The landing

The picture pauses at touchdown, the first moment the foot meets the road. In this data the foot lands ahead of the hips, with the knee bent a little, about 11 to 14 degrees, and the foot nearly flat or heel first. Turn on the forces and the form lines. The arrow shows what the road does to you, and the line follows the shin.

For a moment the road pushes you back. This is braking, and it is small: about 0.2 to 0.4 body weights in two lab datasets. A body weight is the force your own weight puts on the ground, so we use it as a unit. The push upward is much bigger, about two and a half body weights, and you meet it in the next chapter. Halfway through the stance the horizontal push turns forward and helps you on.

Where does the foot land first? On the heel, the middle of the foot or the ball. About 9 in 10 recreational road racers land heel first. Among elite marathon men at the 2017 world championships it was about 55 to 70 percent, and very few landed on the ball. The model runner lands on the heel side, as the average runner in the data does.

Does it matter? No landing style has been shown to be better. Runners of different styles were equally economical. When heel strikers were made to switch, they were less economical for a while. A heel strike gives a sharper first bump in the force than a forefoot strike. Whether that bump matters for injuries is not known.

Now try the slider. It changes the step rate and keeps the speed. Fewer, longer steps put the foot farther ahead and brake more. More, shorter steps land it a little closer. In a lab study of recreational runners at one speed, 10 percent more steps put the heel about 2 cm closer and cut braking by about 16 percent. Fewer steps did the opposite. The model applies that result at all three paces. The rows beside this text show the changes.

Try this: reach a little less, and let the foot land closer to you. Say closer, not "under" the body: in the studies that measured it, the foot still lands a little ahead. How sure is anyone? Short lab tests show less braking with more, shorter steps. In one study of 74 women, the runners with the highest braking forces had more injuries. That shows a link, not a cause.

03

The spring

After touchdown the leg does not stay stiff. The knee bends, the body sinks and the leg pushes back, like a spring with you on top. In this data the knee bends about 43 to 45 degrees at its most. Scrub through the stance, switch on the forces, and watch the arrow grow and fade with the knee and force rows in the gauge.

The road pushes up on you with about 2.3 to 2.6 body weights at the peak, a little more at faster paces. The peak comes around the middle of the stance. Contact lasts about a quarter of a second: 0.23 to 0.30 s by the model's arithmetic, depending on the pace. The force curve in the gauge is the data's own.

Who does the work? In a lab study of ten distance runners, the thigh muscles did most of the braking in the landing, and the calf muscles did most of the pushing. At 5 m/s, one calf muscle, the soleus, pushed up with about 2 body weights by itself.

Tendons help too. The Achilles tendon and the arch of the foot stretch early in the step, store energy and give it back later, like a bouncing ball. That saves your muscles work. Sources disagree on how much, so this page shows no number. The whole leg also gets stiffer as you speed up, mostly at the knee.

Try this: land soft, and let the knee give instead of stiffening against the ground. This is coaching advice. Nobody has tested it well, but it fits the picture of the leg as a spring.

04

Push-off and float

In the second half of the stance the leg pushes you up and forward, and the calf does much of the work. The hip opens, but only a little. In this data the thigh goes just a few degrees behind the line of the pelvis, about 4, 7 and 11 degrees at the three paces. The hip angle in the gauge shows it: a minus sign means the thigh has swung behind you. Then the foot leaves the road.

Now both feet are in the air. The body flies like a thrown ball along a smooth arc. By the model's arithmetic each float lasts about 0.07 to 0.11 s, and there are two floats in every stride. Look from behind and turn on the foot path. It is the track of the right foot as the hip sees it: a loop, not the track over the road.

Your body also bounces. It is lowest near mid-stance and highest in the middle of a float. The model estimates the rise at about 8 cm per step. It gets that number from the measured force, by working out how the force moves the body. Lab studies measured about 8 to 9 cm, so the estimate is in the right place. Switch off the follow camera to see the bounce against the road.

Now the slider again. With more, shorter steps at the same speed, the bounce shrank, by 16 percent for 10 percent more steps in a lab study, and the knee took about a third less energy at landing. With fewer, longer steps the bounce grew. The model's own estimate gives nearly the same change. The study had 45 recreational runners at one speed and short tests. It was not an injury trial.

Try this: quick, light steps instead of long, bouncy ones, and push back enough, not as far as possible. Raising the step rate by 5 to 10 percent lowered knee loading in short lab tests. Most runners already pick a step close to their cheapest, so go gently. One review links less leg extension at toe-off with better economy.

05

Posture and arms

Stand tall, with a slight lean. That is the common advice, and the research behind it is thin. In one study of 24 recreational runners the trunk leaned about 7 degrees forward, and the model uses that as its mean. In another small study the lean swung by a few degrees over each step. It comes from the hips, the pelvis and the trunk together, not from a stiff tilt at the ankles. Nobody knows yet which way more lean helps.

Coaches say to look at the horizon and keep the shoulders relaxed. That is tradition, not a measurement. The model keeps the head level by its own rule. The pelvis is not still. It tips forward and back every step. It drops a little on the side of the swinging leg. It turns a little to each side, and the chest turns against it. Look from above to see the twist. The readout shows the total, an upper estimate. All of this comes from one small study of 19 runners, and the model follows it by rules.

The arms swing against the legs. When the left leg goes forward, the right arm goes forward. This cancels the twist the legs make. In two lab studies the twist of the arms was nearly equal and opposite to the legs', and it doubled from 2 to 5 m/s. The arms do not drive you forward: they add less than 1 percent of the push. Pick the held arms and look from above. The shoulders and the hips twist more.

Do swinging arms save energy? Maybe. In one lab, running with the hands behind the back cost about 3 percent more energy, with the arms across the chest about 9 percent and with the hands on the head about 13 percent. An older study found no change, and another found that the arms act like passive dampers. Scientists disagree, so this page gives no single number.

Coaches teach bent elbows, about a right angle or a little less. Sources say anything from 60 to 90 degrees, and no study has measured a best angle. The size of the arm swing and the elbow angle in the model are its own choices, and nobody has measured them. Use them to see the idea, not as a target.

Try this: run tall with a slight lean, eyes on the horizon and shoulders loose. Let the arms swing forward and back, elbows bent, and not across your chest. How sure is anyone? The tall posture, the loose shoulders and the elbow bend are coaching tradition with thin research. "Not across your chest" has no number of its own, but in one lab, arms held across the chest cost more energy. That the arms cancel the twist of the legs is measured.

06

Pace

Pick each pace in turn. In this data the runner does more of everything: more steps a minute, longer steps, less time on the road, longer floats, a harder push into the ground and a higher knee lift. The rows beside this text show the numbers for each pace. The knee number is the average of each runner's own peak.

Most of the extra speed comes from longer steps, not quicker ones. In one lab, from 2 to 5 m/s, the step rate rose about 21 percent while the step length doubled. So a faster run is mostly a run of longer hops.

You may have heard that 180 steps a minute is the right number. It comes from a coach's count of elite runners at the Olympics, who nearly all stepped faster than that. That story is second-hand, and it is about fast runners at race speed. Step rate rises with speed, and runners differ a lot, from 160 to 210 steps a minute. Many fast runners take 170 to 190. It is a result of running fast, not a target. The rows beside this text give the elite men's numbers.

Do runners pick well? Mostly, yes. In an old lab test, each runner's own step length was close to the one that used the least oxygen, and forcing a longer or shorter step cost energy. A few percent either way costs little. So the slider in the earlier chapters shows what changes. It is not a number to chase.

Running costs about 1 kilocalorie for each kilogram of body per kilometre, at almost any speed. A marathon at 3.5 m/s is about 35 000 of these hops.

The fast pace is close to real racing. At the 2017 world championship marathon, men ran 4.3 to 4.8 m/s with 180 to 184 steps a minute, close to the model's fast pace. The men's marathon record is faster still, at 5.88 m/s.

So what is worth trying? Run tall, with a slight lean and your eyes on the horizon, and let your loose arms swing forward and back. Land a little closer to your body with a shorter reach, and let the knee give like a spring. Take quick, light steps instead of long, bouncy ones. Some of this is measured and some is coaching tradition, and none of it is a promise against injury. The honest bottom line is a friendly one: your body has already found a stride close to its cheapest. So change one small thing at a time, and keep what feels easy.
